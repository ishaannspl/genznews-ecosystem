## The Hook

Most modern hardware startups chase subscription apps and pricey bluetooth add-ons. Five college seniors took the opposite route, designing a dirt-cheap, open-source baseball cap that helps blind users navigate without touching a smartphone.

## TL;DR (Quick Hits)

- Five Rose-Hulman engineering seniors built a battery-powered hat attachment that uses time-of-flight LiDAR sensors to warn visually impaired users of nearby obstacles.
- The device runs for 12 hours on a single charge and is completely self-contained, requiring zero Wi-Fi, data plans, or mobile apps.
- Focus groups with senior citizens taught the team to rethink sensory overload, proving that real accessibility design requires thinking outside your own demographic.

## The Breakdown

The project started with an unexpected insight. John Marum, an alumnus of Rose-Hulman Institute of Technology and founder of the non-profit AuralVision, noticed how automated airport paper towel dispensers use simple time-of-flight sensors to measure distance. He teamed up with five engineering seniors to repurpose that low-cost hardware into an assistive mobility tool.

The final build clips directly onto a standard baseball cap. The sensors scan the space ahead, while haptic motors vibrate against the user's forehead to signal upcoming obstacles that a cane or guide dog might miss. Because everything runs locally on an onboard chip, the device lasts roughly 12 hours without needing an internet connection or buggy firmware updates.

Testing the prototype at an assisted living facility gave the team an immediate reality check. While Gen Z engineers are accustomed to constant digital notifications, older testers found rapid vibrations distracting and headache-inducing. The team documented those design friction points so the next cohort of student engineers can refine the vibration rhythms before moving toward production.

## Why It Matters

Hardware does not need a venture-backed price tag or bloated software to solve a real human problem. If you are building projects to break into engineering or product design, building open-source tools tailored to non-tech-native users is the ultimate portfolio flex.
